Helicopter, air tanker, fire engine, drone — what each is actually for

Four assets that get set against each other. They are not substitutes. They arrive at different stages of the same fire.

18 August 2026 · Averium Dynamics UG, Berlin

The first hour decides it

A forest fire is small for the first hour. After that it is not. What reaches the fire in that hour decides the outcome. Everything after it is damage limitation.

Yet most conversations about firefighting assets are about volume. They should start with time. An engine that arrives after three hours with 4,000 litres is arriving at a different fire from the one that was reported.

This piece compares four assets. Not as competitors, because they are not competitors. A water tender and an air tanker solve different problems at different moments. The question is not which is better. The question is what each is for — and where the gap is.

The criteria, stated openly

So that anyone can check the comparison:

  • 01Time to first suppressant on the fire. Not time to the alarm, not time to leaving the station. Time until water or powder is on the fire.
  • 02Cost per deployment. What one deployment costs, not what the asset costs to buy.
  • 03Terrain it can reach. Where the asset gets to — and where it cannot go, physically or legally.
  • 04Personnel risk. How many people have to enter the hazard area for it to work.
  • 05Suppressant volume. How much arrives per run or per sortie.

A note on criterion 02: this piece contains no euro figures. We could not find defensible, publicly citable per-flight-hour costs for firefighting helicopters or air tankers in Germany. Rather than invent a number, we compare qualitatively here and say where the judgement comes from.

No asset wins on all five. That is the point.

Water tender (Tanklöschfahrzeug, TLF)

The backbone. Unmatched wherever a road goes.

Where a road reaches, the TLF beats everything else. It brings a large volume of water, it can be resupplied, it cools, and the crew can judge the fire from close up and adapt the attack. No aircraft does that. And the aftermath — hot spots, damping down, securing the ground — is done by the crews on the ground regardless.

Honest limitation

The time is not lost on the road. It is lost finding the way.

The biggest delay is not the alarm. It happens finding the route through the terrain. Crews end up searching for the fire themselves — and hours pass before they get there. Paraphrased: a fire officer (Brandamtmann) of the Berlin fire service, 40 years of service, in conversation with us

This matches what crews from forested districts describe. The alarm is fast. The run to the last made-up road is fast. Then the search begins. Forest rescue points (Rettungspunkte) help, but they mark a meeting point, not the seat of the fire.

Firefighting helicopter (Löschhubschrauber)

Decisive on a developed fire. Large volume, fast over distance.

Once a fire is large, everything moves into the air. A helicopter crosses distance and terrain in minutes that would cost a vehicle hours, and it delivers a volume no ground vehicle can bring up in that time. The Müritz fire shows what that means in practice: two German Air Force CH-53s dropped roughly 1,580,000 litres in 316 sorties between 16 and 18 July 2026.[3] The national park administration explicitly credits the helicopter crews with a decisive contribution to containing the fire.[4]

Honest limitation

A drop from altitude is imprecise, and cost limits how often it is flown.

A drop from altitude misses most of the fire. And every flight costs so much that helicopters only get requested in an emergency. Paraphrased: the founder of an aerial firefighting company in Athens, in conversation with us

That is not a criticism of the asset, it is its physics: water falling from height spreads, and part of it evaporates before it arrives. Which is why the helicopter is the asset for the developed fire — not for the first hour, when nobody would call one out.

Air tanker (Löschflugzeug)

The largest volumes. The longest lead time.

Air tankers deliver the largest volume per run of the four and cover large areas. For landscape fires around the Mediterranean they are the backbone of aerial firefighting.

Honest limitation

They need airfields, they need lead time, and they do not fly where a flight restriction applies.

For the first hour of a fire in a German district, the air tanker is effectively not a factor. Requesting, repositioning and loading take time, and the nearest suitable base is rarely close. It is an asset for the situation on day two, not for the first hour.

Drone (Drohne)

Minutes 1 to 15, in terrain nobody is allowed to enter.

We build this one, so we will start with what it cannot do.

Honest limitation

The payload is small. Against a developed front a drone is useless. Dry powder does not cool — re-ignition is a real risk, not a theoretical one.

Send a drone at a crown fire running across 300 hectares and you have already lost the deployment. And powder smothers the flame without cooling the material underneath it. Without follow-up from the ground or the air, it burns again. That is not a footnote. It is the central limitation of the asset.

What does hold:

  • It reaches inaccessible ground fast, with no road and no route-finding.
  • Nobody is in the hazard area.
  • Cost per deployment is orders of magnitude below a flight with a crewed aircraft.
  • It scales by the number of sorties, not by the size of the payload.

That defines a narrow role: the small fire, in the first minutes, at a spot nobody else can reach in that time. Nothing beyond it.

The gap: Müritz National Park, July 2026

On Monday 13 July 2026 a forest fire broke out in Müritz National Park, in the Mecklenburgische Seenplatte district. The ground affected was a former military training area heavily contaminated with unexploded ordnance.

On 15 July the Nordkurier reported the district's position: because a heavily munitions-contaminated former training area was involved, the fire service could not get directly at the fire and had to keep a safety distance of 1,000 metres.[1] That same day, 118 hectares were burning.[1]

It had started at just over 20 hectares. It ended up covering almost 400 hectares[2] — around one per cent of the national park.[4]

What could still fly was the district's own drone unit. Different rules apply to an aircraft that only passes overhead than to responders who have to walk in. Operators from the district's land registry office (Katasteramt) launched from the Käflingsbergturm, a tower south-east of the village of Speck, from six in the morning, often until shortly before midnight. Their job: hold an overview and measure the burnt area.[2]

Reconnaissance only. They could not put anything on the fire.

Two things matter here, and both cut against the convenient version of this story.

First: the helicopters were not shut out. They flew, and they worked — 316 sorties, roughly 1.58 million litres.[3] Anyone claiming that only a drone could fly at Müritz is not telling the truth. The 1,000 metres applied to the crews on the ground.

Second: that is exactly why the gap is visible. For weeks there was ground that no person was permitted to enter. Above that ground there was an uncrewed aircraft more or less continuously — and all it could do was look. The only way to reach the fire itself was from altitude, in sorties with hours between them, at the precision the method allows.

The drone was not the better asset. It was the only asset that could be over the fire in the first hour and stay there — and it had no ability to do anything once it was.

What that means

A drone does not replace a helicopter. It does not replace a water tender. Anyone saying otherwise has not watched either of them work.

It closes a gap that nothing currently covers: the first minutes, at a spot nobody reaches in that time — because no road goes there, or because nobody is allowed to. What comes after is done by the people who have always done it.
