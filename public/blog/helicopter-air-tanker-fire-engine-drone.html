Helicopter, air tanker, fire engine, drone: what each one is for

18 August 2026 · Averium Dynamics UG, Berlin

20 hectares. Then 400.

A fire started in Müritz National Park on 13 July, on old army training ground. When crews first measured it: just over 20 hectares.

By the end it had burned nearly 400. One per cent of the entire park.[2][4]

Nobody made a mistake. Hundreds of people worked it. The German air force dropped 1.58 million litres of water in three days.[3]

It grew anyway.

That fire was small once. What happens in that window decides everything after it. Miss it and you are not fighting a fire any more, you are limiting the damage.

So the question is not which tool carries the most water. It is which tool is there in time.

Our five tests

Out in the open, so you can argue with them:

  • how long until something lands on the fire
  • what one call-out costs
  • where it can go, and where it is allowed to go
  • how many people go into danger
  • how much it carries

On cost: we found no price per flight hour for German firefighting aircraft that we would put our name to. So there are no euro figures here. If we cannot source it, we do not print it.

Nothing wins all five.

Fire engine

Best tool on this list. Where there is a road.

It carries real water. It refills. It cools the ground, not just the flame. The crew see the fire and change the plan while they work. No aircraft does any of that.

Then the road ends.

The biggest delay is not the alarm. It is finding the way through the forest. Crews search for the fire themselves. Hours pass before they arrive. A fire officer in Berlin, forty years of service. Paraphrased.

We have heard that from too many people to call it a one-off. The alarm is fast. The drive is fast. Then it turns into navigation, in a forest, usually at night. Forest rescue points give you somewhere to meet. They do not give you the fire.

Helicopter

Once a fire is big, this is what saves you.

Over Müritz: 316 flights, 1.58 million litres, three days.[3] The park credited the helicopter crews with turning it around.[4]

Water dropped from high up misses most of the fire. And every flight costs so much that helicopters are only called in an emergency. The founder of an aerial firefighting company in Athens. Paraphrased.

Which makes it a tool for a fire that is already out of hand. Nobody scrambles a helicopter for something twenty metres wide. By the time the cost is easy to justify, the cheap version of the problem is long gone.

Air tanker

Biggest load. Longest wait.

Around the Mediterranean it is the backbone of the air response. In a German district, in the first hour, forget it. Request it, move it, load it, fly it. The nearest suitable base is not nearby. This is a day-two tool, and day two is already too late.

Drone

We build these, so we will go first on what they cannot do.

Small tank. Against a real fire front, useless. Send one at 300 hectares of burning canopy and you have wasted the flight and everyone's time.

Dry powder kills the flame without cooling the fuel underneath. So it comes back. Someone still has to walk that ground afterwards.

Müritz again: weeks after the fire was declared under control, drones over the burnt area were still reading 160 degrees.[4] Fires that look finished are not finished.

Now the other side. A drone reaches ground with no road, fast, with nobody hunting for the way in first. Nobody stands in the danger area. One flight costs a fraction of one flight with a pilot on board. And you scale it by flying again, not by building something bigger.

That is a narrow job. Small fire. First minutes. Somewhere nothing else reaches in time.

The gap

Back to Müritz. The size was never the interesting part.

That ground is full of unexploded shells. On 15 July the district said it plainly: crews could not approach the fire. Minimum distance 1,000 metres. That day, 118 hectares were burning.[1]

A thousand metres is not firefighting. It is spectating.

One thing still worked overhead: the district's own drone team. The rules for flying over that ground are not the rules for standing on it. They launched from a tower near Speck, six in the morning until nearly midnight, day after day, watching the fire and measuring how far it had spread.[2]

They could see all of it. They could not touch any of it.

Now the part that goes against us. The helicopters were not grounded. They flew, they worked, 316 sorties, 1.58 million litres.[3] If anyone tells you only a drone could get near Müritz, they are selling you something. The 1,000 metres applied to people on foot.

And that is exactly where the hole is. For weeks there was ground no human could enter. There was an aircraft above it almost continuously, and its entire contribution was looking. Everything that actually hit the fire came from altitude, hours apart. When it was finally declared under control, the bomb disposal service said two more weeks before anyone could set foot on it.[4]

The drone was not the better tool. It was the only tool that could be there in time and stay there. And it arrived with nothing to give.

So

A drone does not replace a helicopter or a fire engine. Anyone who has watched either of them work knows that.

It covers the one window nothing covers: the first minutes, in a place nobody reaches in the first minutes. Because there is no road. Or because nobody is allowed.

Get that window right and most of this article never has to happen.
