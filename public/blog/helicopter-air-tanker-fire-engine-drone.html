Helicopter, air tanker, fire engine, drone: what each one is for

They are not alternatives. They arrive at different times in the same fire, and they do different jobs.

18 August 2026 · Averium Dynamics UG, Berlin

Twenty hectares to four hundred

On 13 July a fire started in Müritz National Park. The land there is an old army training area. When crews first measured the fire it was just over 20 hectares. By the end it had burned nearly 400. That is about one per cent of the park.[2][4]

Nobody made an obvious mistake. Hundreds of people worked on it. The German air force flew two big helicopters and dropped about 1.58 million litres of water in three days.[3] The fire still grew from 20 to 400.

Those two numbers say everything. The fire was small at the start. What you do while it is still small decides how it ends. After that you are only limiting the damage.

People compare firefighting tools by how much water they carry. That is the wrong question. These four are not alternatives. They arrive at different times and do different jobs. Tell a fire crew that drones beat fire engines and they will stop listening. They would be right.

How we compare them

Five points:

  • how long until something reaches the fire
  • what one call-out costs
  • where it can go, and where it is allowed to go
  • how many people have to enter danger
  • how much it carries

About cost: we could not find prices per flight hour for German firefighting helicopters or planes that we trust. So this article has no euro figures. Where cost matters we describe it in words, and we say who told us.

No tool wins on all five points.

Fire engine

Where there is a road, nothing is better. It carries a lot of water. It can refill. It cools the ground, not only the flames. The crew can see the fire and change the plan while they work. No aircraft can do that. And the slow work later, the hot spots and the wetting down, is done on foot anyway.

The problem is not the truck. It is reaching the fire.

The biggest delay is not the alarm. It is finding the way through the forest. Crews have to search for the fire themselves. Hours pass before they arrive. A fire officer in Berlin, forty years of service. Our words.

We have heard this from many people now. The alarm is fast. The drive to the last paved road is fast. Then it becomes navigation, in a forest, often at night. Forest meeting points help, but a meeting point is not the fire.

Helicopter

A helicopter crosses in minutes what takes a truck hours. It brings more water than any vehicle could bring in that time. Müritz shows this: 316 flights and about 1.58 million litres in three days.[3] The park said afterwards that the helicopter crews made the difference.[4]

Water dropped from high up misses most of the fire. And every flight costs so much that helicopters are only called in an emergency. The founder of an aerial firefighting company in Athens. Our words.

This is not a complaint. It is how it works. Water falls, spreads out, and some of it never lands. So a helicopter is for a fire that is already big. Nobody calls one for a fire twenty metres wide.

Air tanker

Planes carry the most water of all. Around the Mediterranean they are the main tool from the air. In a German district, in the first hour, they matter very little. You have to request the plane, move it and load it. That takes time, and the nearest base is usually far away. This is a tool for day two.

Drone

We build these. So here is what ours cannot do.

The tank is small. Against a big fire a drone is useless. Send one at a fire moving through the treetops across 300 hectares and you waste everyone's time. Dry powder stops the flame but does not cool the fuel under it. So the fire can start again, and someone still has to walk the ground later.

Müritz shows this too. Weeks after the fire was under control, drones over the burnt area were still finding hot spots up to 160 degrees.[4] A fire that looks finished is often not finished.

What is true: a drone reaches land with no road, quickly, and nobody has to find the way first. Nobody is in danger. One flight costs a small fraction of a flight with a pilot on board. And you get more by flying again, not by carrying more.

So the job is small and clear. A small fire, in the first minutes, in a place nothing else can reach in that time.

The gap

Back to Müritz. The size is not the interesting part.

The land is an old army training area and it is full of unexploded shells. On 15 July the local paper reported what the district said: crews could not go near the fire. They had to stay 1,000 metres away. That day 118 hectares were burning.[1]

A thousand metres. On foot, that is not firefighting. That is watching.

One thing could still work above it: the drone team of the district. The rules for flying over the land are different from the rules for walking onto it. They flew from a tower southeast of the village of Speck, from six in the morning until close to midnight, day after day. They watched the fire and measured how far it had spread.[2]

They could see everything and change nothing.

Two things follow. The first one is against us.

The helicopters were not blocked. They flew and they worked: 316 flights, 1.58 million litres.[3] If someone tells you that only a drone could get near Müritz, they are selling something. The 1,000 metres was for the crews on the ground.

But this is what makes the gap clear. For weeks there was land nobody could walk on. There was an unmanned aircraft above it almost all the time, and all it could do was watch. Everything that reached the fire came from high up, with hours between flights. When the fire was declared under control, the bomb disposal service said crews could not enter the burnt area for about two more weeks.[4]

The drone was not the better tool. It was the only tool that could be there early and stay there. And it could do nothing once it arrived.

What this means

A drone does not replace a helicopter. It does not replace a fire engine. Anyone who has seen them work knows this.

It fills a gap that nothing fills today. The first minutes, in a place nobody can reach in the first minutes, because there is no road or because nobody is allowed. After that it is the same work as always, done by the same people.
