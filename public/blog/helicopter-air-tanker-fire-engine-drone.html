Helicopter, air tanker, fire engine, drone — what each is actually for

They are not alternatives. They turn up at different points in the same fire, which makes "which one is better" the wrong question to be arguing about.

18 August 2026 · Averium Dynamics UG, Berlin

Twenty hectares to four hundred

On 13 July a fire started in the Müritz National Park, on ground the army used to train on. When it was first sized up it was a bit over 20 hectares. By the end it had covered close to 400 — about one per cent of the whole park.[2][4]

As far as we can tell nobody did anything wrong. Hundreds of people worked it. The Bundeswehr put two CH-53s over it and dropped something like 1.58 million litres across three days.[3] It went from 20 to 400 anyway.

We keep coming back to that pair of numbers. It says more than any argument about equipment does, because the fire was small once, and the stretch where it was still small is where the outcome actually got decided. Most of what happened after that was limiting the damage.

Which is worth keeping in mind when these four things get compared, because they usually get compared on the wrong axis — how much water each one carries. They are not substitutes for each other. They turn up at different points and do different jobs. Tell a Wehr that drones beat fire engines and they will stop listening, and they will be right to.

What we are judging them on

Five things. Better to write them down than leave them implied:

  • how long before something actually lands on the fire
  • what one deployment costs
  • where it can get to, physically and legally
  • how many people have to be inside the hazard area
  • how much it carries

On the second one: we could not find per-flight-hour costs for German firefighting helicopters or air tankers that we would be willing to put our name to. So there are no euro figures anywhere in this piece. Where cost matters we say so in words and say who told us. Making a number up would have been easy and would have undone everything else.

Nothing wins on all five.

Tanklöschfahrzeug

Where there is a road, nothing else is close. A TLF brings real volume, it can be resupplied, it cools rather than just knocking flame down, and the crew can see what they are dealing with and change the attack while they are doing it. No aircraft does that. And whatever else happens, the hot spots, the damping down and the securing of the ground afterwards all belong to the people standing on it.

The limitation is not the vehicle. It is getting the vehicle to the fire.

The biggest delay is not the alarm. It happens finding the route through the terrain. Crews end up searching for the fire themselves — and hours pass before they get there. Paraphrased: a fire officer (Brandamtmann) with the Berlin fire service, 40 years in, talking to us

We have now heard some version of that from enough people that we have stopped treating it as an anecdote. The alarm is quick. The run out to the last piece of tarmac is quick. Then it turns into navigation, in a forest, often at night, sometimes with a reporting party who was not sure where they were either. Forest rescue points (Rettungspunkte) help with the handover, but a rescue point is a place to meet, not the seat of the fire.

Löschhubschrauber

Once a fire is properly going, the work moves into the air. A helicopter covers ground in minutes that would cost a vehicle hours, and it arrives with a load nothing on wheels could have brought up in that time. Müritz is the demonstration: 316 sorties, roughly 1.58 million litres, over three days.[3] The park administration went out of its way afterwards to credit the helicopter crews with a decisive contribution to getting the fire contained.[4]

A drop from altitude misses most of the fire. And every flight costs so much that helicopters only get requested in an emergency. Paraphrased: the founder of an aerial firefighting company in Athens, talking to us

That is physics rather than criticism. Water dropped from height spreads out on the way down and some of it is gone before it lands. It is why the helicopter is the tool for a fire that is already big, and why nobody would call one out for something that is still twenty metres across — by the time the case for the cost has been made, the fire has made it for you.

Löschflugzeug

Air tankers carry more per run than anything else here and cover serious area. Around the Mediterranean they are the backbone of the air response. In a German district, in the first hour, they are close to irrelevant: requesting, repositioning and loading all take time, and the nearest suitable base is rarely nearby. This is a day-two asset. That is the whole entry, and the length of it is the point.

Drohne

We build these, so here is what ours cannot do.

The payload is small. Against a developed front a drone is useless — send one at a crown fire running across 300 hectares and you have wasted the sortie and everyone's time. Dry powder knocks flame down without cooling the fuel underneath it, which means re-ignition is a live problem rather than a theoretical one, and it means somebody still has to come and check the ground afterwards.

Müritz is a good illustration of why that matters, incidentally. Weeks after the fire was contained, drone monitoring over the burn was still picking up individual heat sources at up to 160 °C.[4] Fires that look finished are not finished.

What does hold up: a drone gets to ground with no road to it, quickly, without anyone having to find the way in first. Nobody is in the hazard area. A deployment costs orders of magnitude less than putting a crewed aircraft in the air, which changes what you are willing to send it at. And it scales by flying again rather than by carrying more, which is a different shape of problem to solve.

So the useful role is narrow and specific: a small fire, in the first minutes, somewhere nothing else reaches in that window.

The gap

Back to Müritz, because the interesting part is not the size of it.

The ground was a former military training area and it is heavily contaminated with unexploded ordnance. On 15 July the Nordkurier reported the district's position plainly: because a heavily munitions-contaminated former training area was involved, the fire service could not get directly at the fire and had to keep a safety distance of 1,000 metres. That day 118 hectares were burning.[1]

A thousand metres. On foot, that is not a tactic, it is a spectator position.

What could still work over the top of it was the district's own drone unit. Different rules apply to an aircraft passing overhead than to people who have to walk in. Operators from the district land registry office (Katasteramt) were flying from the Käflingsbergturm, a tower south-east of the village of Speck, from six in the morning until close to midnight, day after day, holding an overview and measuring the burnt area.[2]

They could see everything and do nothing about any of it.

Two things follow from that, and the first one cuts against us.

The helicopters were not shut out. They flew, they were effective, and the record says so — 316 sorties and 1.58 million litres.[3] Anybody telling you that only a drone could get near Müritz is selling something. The 1,000 metres applied to the crews on the ground.

But that is exactly what makes the gap visible. For weeks there was ground no person was allowed to walk onto. There was an uncrewed aircraft over it more or less continuously, and its entire contribution was watching. Everything that actually reached the fire came from altitude, in sorties with hours between them, at whatever accuracy a drop from that height gives you. When the fire was declared under control, the munitions clearance service estimated it would be about two weeks before crews could set foot on the burn at all.[4]

The drone was not the better tool there. It was the only thing that could be over the fire early and stay over it, and it had nothing to offer once it arrived.

So

A drone does not replace a helicopter, and it does not replace a Tanklöschfahrzeug. Anyone who has watched either of them work already knows that.

What it can do is cover a gap that nothing covers at the moment: the first few minutes, somewhere nobody reaches in the first few minutes, either because there is no road or because nobody is permitted. After that it is the same job it has always been, done by the same people.
