Helicopter, air tanker, fire engine, drone: what each one is for

They are not alternatives. They turn up at different points in the same fire and do different jobs.

18 August 2026 · Averium Dynamics UG, Berlin

Twenty hectares to four hundred

On 13 July a fire started in Müritz National Park, on old army training ground. It was just over 20 hectares when crews first sized it up. By the end it had burned close to 400, about one per cent of the park.[2][4]

As far as we can tell nobody did anything wrong. Hundreds of people worked on it. The German air force flew two heavy helicopters over it and dropped around 1.58 million litres in three days.[3] It still went from 20 to 400.

That pair of numbers is the whole argument. The fire was small once. What happens while it is still small decides how it ends. Everything after that is clearing up.

People usually compare firefighting kit by how much water it carries. That is the wrong question. These four things are not alternatives. They arrive at different times and do different jobs. Tell a fire crew that drones beat fire engines and they will stop listening to you, and they should.

How we are comparing them

Five things:

  • how long until something lands on the fire
  • what one call-out costs
  • where it can reach, physically and legally
  • how many people have to go into danger
  • how much it carries

On cost: we could not find flight-hour prices for German firefighting helicopters or planes that we trust enough to print. So there are no euro figures in this piece. Where cost matters we say so in words, and we say who told us.

Nothing wins on all five.

Fire engine

Where there is a road, nothing beats it. It carries a lot of water, it can refill, it cools the ground properly, and the crew can see the fire and change what they are doing. No aircraft can do that. And the slow work afterwards, the hot spots and the damping down, happens on foot anyway.

The problem is not the truck. It is getting the truck to the fire.

The biggest delay is not the alarm. It is finding the route through the terrain. Crews end up searching for the fire themselves, and hours go by before they reach it. A fire officer with the Berlin fire service, forty years in. Our paraphrase.

We have heard that from enough people now to stop calling it an anecdote. The alarm is fast. The drive to the last paved road is fast. Then it turns into navigation, in a forest, often in the dark, sometimes from a caller who was not sure where they were either. Forest meeting points help with the handover, but a meeting point is not the fire.

Helicopter

Once a fire is big, the work moves into the air. A helicopter crosses in minutes what would take a truck hours, and it arrives with a load nothing on wheels could bring in that time. Müritz shows it: 316 flights, around 1.58 million litres, over three days.[3] The park authority later credited the helicopter crews with turning the fire around.[4]

A drop from altitude misses most of the fire. And every flight costs so much that helicopters only get called in an emergency. The founder of an aerial firefighting company in Athens. Our paraphrase.

That is physics, not a complaint. Water dropped from height spreads on the way down and some of it never lands. So a helicopter is for a fire that is already big. Nobody calls one out for something twenty metres across.

Air tanker

Planes carry more per run than anything else here, and around the Mediterranean they are the backbone of the air response. In a German district, in the first hour, they barely matter. Requesting one, moving it and loading it all take time, and the nearest suitable base is rarely close. This is a day-two tool.

Drone

We build these, so here is what ours cannot do.

The load is small. Against a big fire front a drone is useless. Send one at a fire running through the treetops across 300 hectares and you have wasted everyone's time. Dry powder puts the flame out without cooling the fuel underneath, so it can catch again, and somebody still has to walk the ground afterwards.

Müritz shows that too. Weeks after the fire was under control, drone flights over the burn were still finding hot spots at up to 160 degrees.[4] Fires that look finished are not finished.

What is true: a drone reaches ground with no road to it, fast, and nobody has to find the way in first. Nobody is in danger. One flight costs a small fraction of putting a crewed aircraft up, which changes what you are willing to send it at. And you scale it by flying again, not by carrying more.

So the job is narrow. A small fire, in the first few minutes, somewhere nothing else can reach in that time.

The gap

Back to Müritz, because the size of it is not the interesting part.

The ground there is old army training ground and it is full of unexploded shells. On 15 July the local paper reported the district's position: because of that, crews could not go near the fire and had to stay 1,000 metres back. That day 118 hectares were burning.[1]

A thousand metres. On foot that is not a tactic. That is watching.

What could still work over the top was the district's own drone team. Different rules apply to an aircraft passing overhead than to people walking in. They flew from a tower southeast of the village of Speck, from six in the morning until close to midnight, day after day, keeping an eye on the fire and measuring how far it had spread.[2]

They could see all of it and do nothing about any of it.

Two things follow, and the first one goes against us.

The helicopters were not shut out. They flew and they worked: 316 flights, 1.58 million litres.[3] Anyone telling you that only a drone could get near Müritz is selling something. The 1,000 metres applied to the crews on the ground.

But that is what makes the gap easy to see. For weeks there was ground nobody was allowed to walk on. There was an unmanned aircraft above it almost all the time, and all it could do was watch. Everything that actually hit the fire came from high up, in flights with hours between them. When the fire was declared under control, the bomb disposal service said it would be about two weeks before anyone could set foot on the burnt area.[4]

The drone was not the better tool. It was the only thing that could be there early and stay there, and it had nothing to offer once it arrived.

What this means

A drone does not replace a helicopter. It does not replace a fire engine. Anyone who has watched either of them work knows that.

It covers a gap that nothing covers now. The first few minutes, somewhere nobody can reach in the first few minutes, because there is no road or because nobody is allowed. After that it is the same job it has always been, done by the same people.
